How a fox-hunt fits in 320×240 pixels

Beestenjacht is a creature-catching game for the Fri3d 2026 badge — kids hunt hidden radio beacons in a forest with a directional antenna and collect them in a Pokédex-style book. One Python codebase runs on the badge hardware and on a macOS emulator. This page builds up how it works, one primitive at a time, with figures you can poke at.

1. What is the player actually doing?

The game restages a real radio sport. In ARDF — amateur radio direction finding, the "fox hunt" — small transmitters are hidden over a field, and you carry a directional antenna to home in on them. Beestenjacht dresses each transmitter as a cartoon "beest" (Dutch for beast). You can't see the beasts; you can only feel them through signal strength: turn until the signal peaks, walk until it grows, and when you reach the box you read a 4-digit code off its back and type it into the badge to catch the creature.

There is no map and no compass on screen — that's the whole sport. The only feedback is "warmer / colder." Drag the badge around the field below and watch the meter.

That "warmer / colder" loop is the spine of the whole app. Everything that follows — the silhouette you chase, the heartbeat that races, the five LEDs that fill — exists to communicate that one scalar: how close are you? The catch itself is a reward screen. The hunt is the game.

2. The same code runs on a badge and on your laptop

An ESP32-S3 badge with a soldered antenna is a poor place to iterate on UI. So the app is written once and runs unchanged in two places: on the badge, and on a macOS SDL emulator of MicroPythonOS. The trick is that the app never touches a pin directly. Hardware lives behind a board layer — board/fri3d_2026.py on the badge, board/linux.py on the desktop — that brings up the 320×240 display, input, LEDs and audio for whichever machine you're on.

Where a feature only exists on real hardware — the five NeoPixels, the piezo buzzer — the app calls anyway, checks whether the manager is available, and falls back. Flip the target below to see which calls light up real hardware and which quietly no-op.

As you can see, on the desktop the LED call simply returns False and the buzzer find returns nothing, so play() stays silent — and the hunt screen's on-screen five-LED mirror takes over the job the physical strip would have done. That mirror isn't a debug crutch; it ships on the badge too, so a player whose eyes are on the screen still sees the hot/cold meter. One call site, two honest outcomes.

This "call, check, fall back" rule appears three times in the codebase — for LEDs, for sound, and (as we'll see) for the radio itself. It's the single idea that lets one file tree target two machines.

3. Why every coordinate is a whole number

A web mockup can say "this column is 46% wide" and the browser will happily paint half a pixel. A 320×240 framebuffer cannot — each element must land on an integer pixel or it smears. So the project's source of truth for geometry isn't the code; it's layout/beestenjacht-layout.html, a pixel-exact mock snapped to a grid where every size is a whole number. Those integers become the literal arguments to LVGL's set_pos(x, y) and set_size(w, h).

The home grid is the clearest case: a 4×3 book of creatures, each cell 74×66 px with a 4 px gap and 6 px side margins. Toggle the 8-pixel raster and the column math below.

Turn on the math and you can see why the numbers aren't arbitrary: four 74-px cells, three 4-px gaps, two 6-px margins sum to exactly 320. Nothing is fractional, so nothing blurs. This is also why the plan insists on doing the layout first — you compute the snapped integers in the mock, then transcribe them, rather than nudging set_pos by eye on the device.

The trade-off is rigidity: change one cell width and you re-do the arithmetic for the whole row. The app accepts that, because for repeated rows it doesn't hand-place at all — which is the next idea.

4. The look lives in one place

Two disciplines keep the UI consistent and the badge's RAM happy. First, every colour and spacing value is a named token in ui.py — never a raw 0x… in a screen. Here is the actual palette:

Second — and this is the part specific to LVGL — repeated visual properties go into a shared style object, not onto each widget. In LVGL, every time you call a set_style_* setter inline, the property is stored in that one widget's private local-style store. Do it on a hundred widgets and you've paid for a hundred copies of the same fact. Define one lv.style_t and add_style() it instead, and all hundred widgets point at a single shared record.

Drag the slider to add widgets and watch the cost diverge.

Notice how the red bar climbs linearly while the blue stays flat at one. On a desktop that's noise; on an ESP32-S3 with a screen full of pixel sprites it's the difference between fitting in RAM and not. The five shared styles in ui.py — _RESET, _PANEL, _SEG_CELL, _FOCUS, _PRESSED — carry the reset, the ink outline, the hairline, the gold focus ring and the press-nudge for every widget that wants them. Only genuinely per-widget values (one cell's background colour) stay inline.

5. A creature drawn from a string

There are twelve beasts, but not twelve image files. Each creature is one of four 16×16 shapes — fox, owl, deer, bird — written as a grid of characters, recoloured by one of eight palettes. Four shapes × eight palettes covers all twelve with no art assets at all. Here is the fox, exactly as it appears in art.py:

To draw it, the renderer walks the grid and, for each character that isn't a dot, looks up a colour in the palette and paints a scale × scale block onto an LVGL canvas. The palette is where the magic happens: only r (body), d (dark) and l (light) change per creature; outline, eyes and nose are shared. Pick a shape, a palette and a scale below — this canvas runs the same block-fill the badge does.

Drag the scale and the sprite stays razor-sharp at every size — because each source pixel is a whole block, never interpolated. Flip on silhouette and every visible block collapses to one dark colour: that's how an un-caught creature hides while keeping its exact outline. The whole module is deliberately the "art swap point" — a creature can opt into a real 16×16 PNG by naming an "img" field, and every screen picks it up unchanged, drawn with antialiasing off so the pixels stay crisp.

6. The catch is the reveal

A design rule runs through the whole game: you never see a creature in full until you catch it. On the home book it's a silhouette labelled ???; while you type the code it fills in from the top, a quarter per digit; on the win screen it's finally whole. One parameter drives all of that — a reveal fraction from 0 to 1. Rows above the cutoff get their real colour; rows below stay silhouette.

Slide it from 0 to 4 and watch the colour pour down. Mechanically this is cheap: the renderer draws the silhouette base, then a colour copy clipped to the top reveal fraction. Emotionally it's the entire payoff — the creature you've been chasing as a featureless shadow resolves into itself exactly as you finish entering its code. The reveal isn't decoration layered on top of the catch; the reveal is what catching looks like.

7. Screens are Activities, and they form a loop

MicroPythonOS borrows Android's model: each screen is an Activity with lifecycle hooks (onCreate, onResume, onPause), and you move between them by firing an Intent that names the next Activity class plus some extras — here, almost always {"fox_id": …}. Activities stack: starting one pushes it on top; going back pops it.

There are two journeys through that stack. The catch loop — Home → Hunt → Code → Win — and the care loop a caught creature unlocks — Home → Beest → Feed / Dossier. Step through the figure to watch the stack grow and shrink.

Notice the asymmetry at the end. The hunt and code screens are reached one push at a time, but the win screen doesn't want you walking back through "enter the code" for a creature you've already caught — so go_home() pops the three screens in one move (for _ in range(3): back_screen()) and drops you on the book. There's also no app-drawn back button anywhere: the system owns "back" via the left-edge swipe, Esc, or the joystick, and every app honours that. One more subtlety — Home's onResume rebuilds its tiles with clean() rather than a second setContentView, because re-setting the view would leak the old screen and all eleven of its canvas buffers.

8. A seam where the real radio will land

The actual LoRa direction-finding is someone else's job. The app programs against an interface — FoxRadio, with three methods: which beacons are transmitting, what's the current reading for one, and does this code match. Today a FakeFoxRadio implements it and drives the whole UI with no hardware; when the real radio arrives it implements the same three methods and one line swaps the RADIO singleton.

The fake earns its keep with one honest design choice: there is no "found" flag. Radio signal strength can tell you you're close, but it can't tell you you've physically reached the box — so the reading reports only a continuous strength and a discrete 0–5 level, and the player decides when to enter the code. The fake's strength drifts upward with noise the longer you search, so on the desktop a hunt "warms up" on its own in a few seconds, exercising every screen without a single radio packet.

9. The hunt, assembled and live

Now the pieces combine. Every 250 ms the hunt screen asks the radio for a reading and turns that one number into three synchronised cues: a beats-per-minute readout (60 + strength·100), the count of lit LEDs (round(strength·5)), and a heart that nudges up and down each tick so it visibly throbs. Below is the hunt screen rebuilt from the real layout. Drag the strength, or press play to let the fake radio drift the way it does on the desktop.

Press play and watch all three cues move together as the strength climbs — bpm rises, LEDs fill left-to-right, the heart speeds up. That single-source-of-truth design is why the on-screen mirror and the physical strip can never disagree: both read colors_for_level(level), so the colour of LED #3 is decided in exactly one place. The screen deliberately never declares "found"; it just keeps reporting closeness until you tap VOER DE CODE IN yourself.

10. Catching it, and keeping it after a reboot

The code screen is where the reveal from §6 and the radio seam from §8 meet. Each key fills another quarter of the creature; on the fourth digit (or on OK) the entry is checked with RADIO.verify_code. Right code: the creature is written to persistent storage and you advance to the win screen. Wrong code: the entry clears, the creature re-hides, and you try again — forgiving, never punishing. Try it on the live keypad (the Vos's code is 1234).

Persistence is deliberately tiny. store.py keeps a single list under the key caught in a per-app SharedPreferences file (data/com.fri3d.beestenjacht/config.json) — the same path on badge and desktop. Catching appends an id; the home book reads the list back to decide which tiles are revealed and to draw its 4/12 counter. No network is needed for the save, and a re-catch never duplicates an id. That JSON file is also where each creature's living state goes — which is the last idea.

11. A creature that keeps living while you're away

A caught creature isn't a trophy; it's a small Tamagotchi. It has four living stats — binding (loyalty), mood, energy, hunger — and they drift while the badge is off. The rules are a single pure module, pet.py: no LVGL, no hardware, just maths, so it can be unit-tested with uv run pet.py. The store stamps the wall-clock; pet.py ages the stats by however many real hours have passed.

Drag "hours away" to age a freshly-caught creature, then act on it. Some actions are refused for personality — a full creature won't eat, a tired one won't play — so the stats genuinely constrain what you can do.

Watch hunger climb and energy drain as the hours pass, then feed it and see hunger fall back. The one stat that never decays is binding — loyalty is permanent, which is what turns levels (every 20 points of binding is a level) into a long-game reward. Push energy below 20 and try spelen: it refuses with "te moe om te spelen." Because the module is pure, those refusal rules are checked by the self-test at the bottom of the file — the game's personality is the part that's actually verified.

12. The whole map

Every primitive is now in hand, so the full architecture reads cleanly. At the centre is a pure core — creatures.py (the roster) and pet.py (the rules) — that depends on nothing and runs anywhere. A UI layer (ui.py, art.py) turns LVGL into the app's vocabulary. Screens compose both. And four backend seams — radio, LEDs, sound, store — each hide a machine difference behind an interface, exactly the pattern from §2 and §8.

The shape that recurs at every scale is the same: decide a thing in exactly one place. One layout mock owns the geometry; one token owns each colour; one shared style owns each repeated property; one colors_for_level owns the LED colours; one interface owns each hardware difference. That discipline is what lets a game with twelve creatures, six screens and a living-companion system fit on a microcontroller and still run on your laptop from the same source.
